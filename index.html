How to read it. ψnlm = Rnl(r)·Ylm(θ,φ) for a one-electron atom with nuclear charge Z. n sets the size and energy, l the shape (s, p, d, f…), mₗ the orientation. Real orbitals mix +mₗ and −mₗ into the familiar px, dxy shapes, colored by the sign of ψ. Bomb Calorimeter

Burn a sample at constant volume and read the heat from the temperature rise of the water.

Sample

Calorimeter

Calculation

How it works. The sample sits in a sealed steel “bomb” filled with excess O2 at about 30 atm. A current through the fuse wire ignites it, and the heat passes through the bomb wall into the stirred water. The volume is fixed, so no P–V work is done and the heat released is qv = ΔU. The calorimeter is insulated, so qrxn = −Ccal·ΔT, where Ccal covers the water and the bomb together. Convert to enthalpy with ΔH = ΔU + ΔngasRT. With Heat loss to room on, the trace drifts back toward 25 °C and the peak ΔT underestimates the heat; real experiments correct for this by extrapolating the drift lines back to the ignition time.

Data. Standard enthalpies of combustion at 298 K with H2O(l) as product (NIST Chemistry WebBook, CRC Handbook). The heat from the fuse wire and from nitric-acid formation is ignored.

Electron bookkeeping

Shape (VSEPR)

Display

How it works. The formula gives the skeleton: the least electronegative atom goes in the middle, H and halogens sit on the outside, and in oxoacids H bonds to O. Every combination of single, double and triple bonds is then tried. Lone pairs go first to the outer atoms, then to the central atom. The structure kept is the one where every atom has an octet (H: 2, B: 6), with the smallest formal charges, and any negative charge on the most electronegative atom. When several placements tie, they are the resonance structures.

Formal charge = valence electrons − lone-pair electrons − ½ bonding electrons. Expanded octets are a bookkeeping choice: for ions like SO42− some textbooks keep the octet and accept +2 on S, while others add S=O bonds to cancel it. Real bonding in these ions is closer to the octet picture, so the toggle starts off. Lewis structures also can't show that O2 has two unpaired electrons.

Polarity

Each bond dipole arrow points to the more electronegative atom (δ−). The crossed tail marks the δ+ end, and the length is the electronegativity difference ΔEN. Lone pairs on inner atoms add a dipole along their direction. If all the arrows cancel, as in CO2, BF3, CCl4 and XeF4, the molecule is nonpolar even though its bonds are polar. Bond types: ΔEN below 0.4 is nonpolar covalent, up to 1.8 is polar covalent. CO and O3 show where this simple model breaks down.

Display

Set n and m builds a generic AXnEm: a central atom A with n bonded atoms X and m lone pairs. Change either count and watch the domains rearrange. From a formula gets the counts from a real Lewis structure.

How it works. The central atom's electron domains come from its bonds and lone pairs: each bonded atom counts once, whether the bond is single, double or triple, and so does each lone pair. The domains repel and settle as far apart as possible: a line, triangle, tetrahedron, trigonal bipyramid, octahedron or pentagonal bipyramid. That is the electron geometry. The molecular shape names only where the atoms are. In a trigonal bipyramid, lone pairs take the roomier equatorial sites, which is why AX4E is a seesaw and AX3E2 is T-shaped.

Lone-pair squeeze pushes the bonds away from the lone pairs, to angles typical of each class: AX3E ≈ 107°, AX2E2 ≈ 104.5°, AX4E ≈ 102°/173°, AX3E2 ≈ 87.5°, AX5E ≈ 85°. Real molecules differ by a few degrees. In formula mode, for molecules with several centres, pick one central atom at a time; atoms marked … continue into the rest of the molecule.

How it works. In valence bond theory, each bond is a pair of electrons shared in the overlap of one orbital from each atom. More overlap gives a stronger bond. To make its bonds point the right way, an atom can hybridize: it mixes its s and p (and d) orbitals into equivalent hybrids. That needs, when there aren't enough unpaired electrons, promoting an electron to a higher orbital first. Head-on overlap along the bond axis gives a σ bond. Side-on overlap of parallel p orbitals gives a π bond, which sits above and below the axis. A double bond is σ + π, and a triple bond is σ + 2π.

Shapes. Textbook draws each lobe as a teardrop, as in textbook figures. Computed uses the same hybrid and atomic coefficients with hydrogen-like radial functions (Clementi effective nuclear charges; n = 3 functions stand in for Br and Xe) and draws the surface enclosing 90 % of the electron; bonding orbitals are φA + φB at real bond lengths (H₂, HF, F₂, C–C 1.54 Å, C=C 1.34 Å, C≡C 1.20 Å; X–H, X–F). Pink and blue are the two phases of ψ (the sign, not charge). The energy curve is schematic.

How it works. When two atoms approach, each pair of matching atomic orbitals combines in two ways. In phase (ψA + ψB) gives a lower-energy bonding orbital with density between the nuclei. Out of phase (ψA − ψB) gives a higher-energy antibonding orbital (*) with a node between them. Electrons fill the molecular orbitals from the bottom up, two per orbital, spreading over equal-energy orbitals before pairing (Hund's rule). Bond order = ½ (bonding − antibonding electrons); 0 means no stable molecule.

Shapes are computed: ψ = φA ± φB from hydrogen-like 1s, 2s and 2p wavefunctions with Clementi effective nuclear charges, at the experimental bond length, drawn as the surface that encloses 90 % of the electron probability (in General mode, using the period-2 atom with that many valence electrons). Pink is +, blue is −. This is the simple LCAO picture: it leaves out 2s–2p mixing in the σ orbitals. The energy levels are schematic, showing order, not exact values.
